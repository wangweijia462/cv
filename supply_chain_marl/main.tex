%% ── abstract ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
Coordinating multi-tier supply chains under disruption requires jointly managing
private information across tier boundaries, non-stationary demand shocks, and
heterogeneous data capabilities---a combination that single-agent and classical heuristic
methods handle poorly.  We propose CCD-MAPPO, a multi-agent reinforcement learning
framework for cooperative supply chain resilience built on three principled innovations.
First, a data-element Coupling Coordination Degree index is embedded into agents'
observations and team reward.  No symmetric linear feature $\alpha B+\beta D$ can
distinguish the balance dimension this index encodes when the optimal policy depends
non-additively on breadth--depth balance; ablation confirms this empirically and
Proposition~1 formalizes it.
Second, a residual-policy construction anchored on an $(s,S)$ inventory heuristic
under centralized training and decentralized execution bounds worst-case performance
at the classical baseline and preserves B2B information privacy at execution time, with
a formal safety guarantee proved in Proposition~2.
Third, a linear-scaling graph neural network critic reduces parameter complexity from
$O(|\mathcal{N}|^2)$ to $O(|\mathcal{E}|)$ for industrial-size networks, as established
in Proposition~3.
Against five baselines over four disruption scenarios with five random seeds,
CCD-MAPPO achieves a mean return of $-7734.6\pm312.4$---the best within the
decentralized-execution class, improving 4.1\% over vanilla MAPPO, 8.3\% over QMIX,
and 23.7\% over the heuristic, all confirmed at $p<0.05$ by Wilcoxon rank-sum tests.
Ablation confirms CCD is non-redundant with its components, and reward-weight
sensitivity is stable across a two-octave parameter sweep.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Keywords:} Multi-agent reinforcement learning; supply chain resilience;
coupling coordination degree; residual policy; centralized training decentralized
execution; graph neural network.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The fragility of global supply chains has received unprecedented attention following the
COVID-19 pandemic, the Russia--Ukraine conflict, and recurrent climatic extremes that
simultaneously exposed demand-side volatility and supply-side brittleness
\citep{ivanov2020viable,hosseini2019review}.  Three structural shifts make the resilience challenge qualitatively different from
classical inventory management.
(i) \emph{Decision decentralization}: no single planner can observe upstream lead times,
mid-stream capacity, and downstream demand simultaneously; B2B contracts routinely
prohibit cross-tier state sharing.
(ii) \emph{Non-stationary disruption dynamics}: even modest shocks amplify through the
bullwhip effect into chain-wide stockouts, so recovery requires coordinated ordering
revisions across tiers---not just local adjustments by individual agents who lack
visibility into how their decisions propagate upstream and downstream.
(iii) \emph{Data-element heterogeneity}. Since data was formally recognized as a
``fifth factor of production'' \citep{liao1999quantitative}, firms have diverged sharply
in \emph{how} they exploit data: some invest broadly across many sources; others go deep
on fewer.  This structural gap in data capability---captured by the breadth--depth
trade-off---shapes disruption response speed in ways that aggregate digitalization
indices obscure \citep{sheffi2005resilient,hendricks2005empirical}.

Three lines of prior work each address part of the problem while leaving another part
open.  Econometric methods (panel data, difference-in-differences) can identify
\emph{whether} digitalization improves resilience, but they treat firm decisions as a
reduced-form residual and cannot prescribe real-time ordering adjustments.
Operations-research heuristics---the $(s,S)$ rule, rolling-horizon LP---are prescriptive
but assume stationarity and a single rational planner, which is increasingly unrealistic
for multi-tier networks where no single actor controls the full supply chain.  Single-agent
DRL handles non-stationarity, yet it ignores strategic interdependence and cannot respect
the B2B constraint that proprietary state must not be shared across tier boundaries.
What is missing is a framework that simultaneously handles partial observability,
multi-tier strategic interaction, and structural heterogeneity in data capability---which
is what this paper attempts.

Recent progress in multi-agent RL \citep{lowe2017multi,yu2022surprising,rashid2018qmix}
provides a natural language for this problem, but two gaps persist.  First, prior MARL
applications to supply chains \citep{oroojlooyjadid2022deep,zhang2020multi} include
digital capability only as a scalar endowment, ignoring the \emph{structural
composition} of that capability (breadth vs.\ depth).  Second, the coupling between
breadth and depth---a key result from coupling-coordination theory
\citep{liao1999quantitative}---has not been operationalized within an RL state space,
despite its empirical importance in explaining cross-firm heterogeneity in disruption
recovery.

We formulate cooperative supply chain resilience as a partially observable Multi-Agent
Markov Decision Process (M-MDP) and propose \textbf{CCD-MAPPO}: a MAPPO algorithm
augmented by a data-element Coupling Coordination Degree signal.  Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{C\arabic*.},leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Representational efficiency of CCD.}  Under an empirically verified
  condition on the supply chain environment (Condition A2, confirmed by ablation in
  Section~\ref{sec:ablation}), we show that CCD is the canonical scalar in its
  function class for capturing the non-additive interaction between data breadth
  and depth that no symmetric linear combination $\alpha B+\beta D$ can recover
  (Proposition~1).
  This provides a principled feature-engineering justification beyond ad hoc construction.

\item \textbf{Safe residual policy with provable baseline guarantee.}  We prove that the
  residual-policy construction initializes at the $(s,S)$ baseline and that the learned
  deviation from it is bounded at all times (Proposition~2), enabling deployment without
  risk of catastrophic early-training failures.

\item \textbf{Scalable GNN critic and empirical evaluation.}  A graph-neural-network
  formulation reduces critic parameter complexity from quadratic to linear
  (Proposition~3), validated by zero-shot transfer to 21-agent networks.  The full
  evaluation spans five baselines---$(s,S)$, IPPO, MADDPG, QMIX, oracle DQN---four
  disruption scenarios, and five random seeds, with Wilcoxon significance tests and a
  five-component ablation study.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:related} surveys supply-chain resilience measurement, MARL algorithms,
and coupling-coordination theory.
Section~\ref{sec:theory} presents the formal framework, theoretical propositions, and the
algorithm.
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} reports the empirical evaluation, ablation, sensitivity
analysis, and scalability study.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes with managerial and policy implications.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Supply Chain Resilience Measurement}

Quantifying supply chain resilience requires bridging two traditions that have historically
proceeded in parallel: normative frameworks that define what resilience means, and
operational metrics that measure it.  \citet{bruneau2003framework} establish the canonical
four-R taxonomy---Robustness, Redundancy, Resourcefulness, and Rapidity---operationalized
through a normalized service-level trajectory $F(t)\in[0,1]$.  This framework distinguishes
performance degradation from recovery speed, a distinction that matters for multi-tier
networks because the two phases have different managerial levers.
\citet{christopher2004building} sharpen this by separating \emph{resistance} (avoiding
performance loss before disruption) from \emph{recovery} (restoring performance after), and
argue that organizational agility---the ability to respond quickly to unanticipated
changes---is the critical determinant of the recovery phase.

The operationalization of these concepts for quantitative analysis has been surveyed
comprehensively by \citet{hosseini2019review}, who identify three metrics with the broadest
empirical and analytical support: the cumulative resilience loss $\mathcal{L}=\int(1-F(t))\,dt$,
the time-to-recover $T_R$, and the minimum service level $F_{\min}$.  A key finding of
that survey is that no single metric captures both the depth and duration of a disruption;
using $\mathcal{L}$ and $T_R$ jointly provides a more complete picture than either alone.
We adopt both in our evaluation.

\citet{sheffi2005resilient} argues from an industry perspective that resilience is built
before disruptions occur through investments in flexibility---supplier diversification,
cross-trained workforces, and modular process design.  This pre-disruption investment view
is consistent with our treatment of data-capability indices ($B$ and $D$) as time-varying
states that reflect ongoing firm-level investments rather than fixed parameters.
\citet{ivanov2020viable} extends this to long-duration shocks through the Viable Supply
Chain model, which requires survivability (continuing to operate under persistent stress)
in addition to recoverability---motivating our compound-shock scenario S4.
\citet{dolgui2021ripple} study how local disruptions cascade into chain-wide failures
through the ripple effect; their analysis informs our bullwhip penalty, which discourages
order-quantity amplification that would propagate local shocks upward.

Despite this wealth of frameworks, a structural gap remains: existing measurement
approaches treat resilience as an observable outcome to be estimated ex post, but do not
prescribe how ordering policies should be adapted in real time under partial observability.
Converting resilience metrics into a reward signal that agents can optimize---while
respecting the B2B privacy constraints that prevent full state sharing---is the optimization
problem this paper addresses.

\subsection{Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning}

\subsubsection*{Foundations: from single-agent DRL to cooperative MARL}

Single-agent deep reinforcement learning was placed on firm empirical ground by
\citet{mnih2015human}, who showed that a deep Q-network can achieve human-level performance
on complex sequential tasks by approximating $Q$-values with convolutional neural networks.
The policy-gradient perspective was advanced by \citet{schulman2016high}, who introduced
Generalized Advantage Estimation to reduce the variance of policy gradient estimates, and
by \citet{schulman2017proximal}, whose Proximal Policy Optimization algorithm constrains
each gradient step through a clipped surrogate objective, enabling stable learning in
high-dimensional continuous action spaces.  These single-agent foundations underpin our
choice of PPO-based MAPPO as the backbone algorithm.

Extending single-agent RL to multiple interacting agents introduces the non-stationarity
problem: from any single agent's perspective, the environment appears non-stationary because
the policies of other agents change throughout training.  The Centralized Training with
Decentralized Execution (CTDE) paradigm resolves this by allowing agents to share information
during training while acting independently at deployment.  \citet{lowe2017multi} introduce
MADDPG, the first CTDE actor-critic architecture, with centralized critics conditioned on
joint observations and actions.  \citet{rashid2018qmix} propose QMIX, a value-decomposition
approach that factorizes the joint $Q$-function through a monotone mixing network, achieving
strong performance on cooperative benchmarks while preserving decentralized execution.
\citet{papoudakis2021benchmarking} conduct a large-scale benchmark and demonstrate that
Independent PPO (IPPO), despite using no shared information at all, remains a surprisingly
competitive baseline---raising the bar for what CTDE methods must demonstrate to justify
their coordination overhead.  \citet{yu2022surprising} show that MAPPO, equipped with a
centralized critic trained on the joint state, achieves state-of-the-art sample efficiency
among cooperative CTDE methods.

\subsubsection*{MARL in supply chain and logistics}

\citet{oroojlooyjadid2022deep} establish the first DRL benchmark for multi-tier inventory
optimization through a deep Q-network applied to the Beer Distribution Game, demonstrating
that learned policies can outperform the order-up-to heuristic under demand uncertainty.
\citet{zhang2020multi} demonstrate MARL-based cooperative scheduling gains in
computer-integrated manufacturing, where agents coordinate machine assignments to minimize
makespan.  \citet{holler2019deep} apply multi-agent DRL to vehicle dispatching and
repositioning, finding that CTDE consistently outperforms independent learners in
congestion-prone settings---an analogy for the order-amplification bottlenecks in supply
chains.  None of these works incorporates structural data-capability heterogeneity (breadth
vs.\ depth of data use) into the state or reward, which is the gap our framework addresses.

\subsubsection*{Graph-structured critics and safe policy learning}

The supply chain network is naturally a graph, motivating graph-neural-network (GNN)
representations for the centralized value function.  \citet{hamilton2017inductive}
introduce GraphSAGE, which generates inductive node embeddings by aggregating neighbor
features, enabling generalization to unseen graph topologies.  \citet{velic2018graph}
extend this with Graph Attention Networks, which learn importance weights for each
neighbor via multi-head attention, providing a principled mechanism for selective
information aggregation.  We build our scalable GNN critic on these foundations,
replacing the quadratic fully-connected value head with a message-passing architecture
that achieves $O(|\mathcal{E}|)$ parameter complexity.

For safe policy learning, \citet{silver2018residual} propose residual RL, in which a
learned policy adds a correction term to an existing controller, guaranteeing that the
combined policy cannot be worse than the base controller at initialization.  We adapt
this idea to the multi-agent CTDE setting, extend it with a formal deviation bound
that holds at every training stage (not only at initialization), and prove that the
construction preserves B2B information privacy at execution time---properties not
established in the original residual-RL framework.

\subsection{Coupling Coordination Degree Theory}

Coupling coordination analysis, adapted to social science by \citet{liao1999quantitative},
provides a compact scalar measure for the synergy between two interacting subsystems.  The
framework decomposes the relationship between two variables $B$ and $D$ into two orthogonal
components.  The \emph{coupling degree}
\[
  C(B,D) = \frac{2\sqrt{BD}}{B+D}
\]
equals the ratio of the harmonic mean to the arithmetic mean---a quantity that equals 1
when $B=D$ and decreases monotonically as the ratio $B/D$ departs from unity.  It thus
captures structural \emph{balance} between the two subsystems, irrespective of their
absolute levels.  The \emph{synergy index}
\[
  T = \alpha B + \beta D, \quad \alpha + \beta = 1,
\]
is a weighted linear combination that captures the joint \emph{level}.  The Coupling
Coordination Degree $\CCD = \sqrt{C \cdot T}$ combines both dimensions into a single
scalar: it is high when the two subsystems are simultaneously well-balanced and
high-performing, and low when either dimension is deficient.

This formulation was developed in the context of environment--economy coordination
\citep{liao1999quantitative} and has since been applied to regional development planning,
energy-economy coupling assessment, and urban sustainability index construction.  A key
property that makes CCD preferable to simpler aggregates such as $\sqrt{BD}$ (geometric
mean) is its \emph{explicit factorization}: $C$ and $T$ are separately interpretable as a
balance score and a level score, respectively, allowing analysts to diagnose the source of
low coordination---whether it arises from a breadth--depth mismatch (low $C$) or from
insufficient capability overall (low $T$).  This diagnostic property is valuable in
policy contexts where targeted interventions require knowing \emph{which} dimension to
address.

Despite CCD's widespread use in economic and geographic measurement, its potential as a
state feature for reinforcement learning agents has not been explored.  Prior MARL supply
chain work treats firm-level capability as a scalar or omits it entirely; the structural
composition of that capability---how breadth and depth combine---has not been incorporated
into either the state representation or the reward signal.  We provide the first
representational-efficiency argument for why CCD belongs in the state directly (rather
than being derived from separate $B$ and $D$ observations by the agent's neural network):
symmetric linear features $\alpha B + \beta D$ cannot recover the balance non-linearity
that CCD encodes, a claim formalized in Proposition~1 and confirmed empirically by
controlled ablation.

\subsection{Multi-Agent MDP Formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

We model the supply chain as a partially observable M-MDP following the Markov game
framework of \citet{lowe2017multi}:
\begin{equation}
\M = \bigl\langle \N,\,\cS,\,\{A_i\}_{i\in\N},\,\mathcal{P},\,\{R_i\}_{i\in\N},\,
                  \{\Omega_i\}_{i\in\N},\,\gamma \bigr\rangle,
\label{eq:mmdp}
\end{equation}
where the agent set $\N = \N_s\cup\N_m\cup\N_d$ is partitioned into $|\N_s|=3$ suppliers,
$|\N_m|=2$ manufacturers, and $|\N_d|=4$ distributors (Figure~\ref{fig:topology}).  The
transition kernel $\mathcal{P}(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,\mathbf{a}_t)$ is unknown to all agents,
capturing exogenous disruption stochasticity $\xi_t$.  Each agent $i$ observes only
$\Omega_i\subseteq\cS$, modeling tier-specific information asymmetry.  The discount factor
$\gamma\in(0,1)$ governs the planning horizon.

\subsection{State Space with Coupling Coordination Degree}
\label{sec:statespace}

The global state at time $t$ is
\begin{equation}
s_t = \bigl[\,\mathbf{I}_t,\;\mathbf{T}_t,\;\mathbf{D}_t,\;\mathbf{C}_t,\;
             \mathbf{B}_t,\;\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{pth}}_t,\;\mathbf{CCD}_t,\;\boldsymbol{\xi}_t
       \,\bigr]^\top,
\label{eq:state}
\end{equation}
with inventory $\mathbf{I}_t\in\R^{|\N|}$, in-transit goods $\mathbf{T}_t$, demand
$\mathbf{D}_t$, capacity utilization $\mathbf{C}_t$, agent-level data-breadth
$\mathbf{B}_t\in[0,1]^{|\N|}$, data-depth $\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{pth}}_t\in[0,1]^{|\N|}$,
the coupling-coordination vector $\mathbf{CCD}_t\in[0,1]^{|\N|}$, and exogenous shock
signals $\boldsymbol{\xi}_t$.  Each agent's local observation is
$o_{i,t} = s_t \odot \mathbf{m}_i$, where $\mathbf{m}_i\in\{0,1\}^{\dim(s)}$ is a
tier-specific visibility mask encoding the information asymmetry inherent in B2B
relationships.

For each agent $i$ at time $t$:
\begin{equation}
\CCD_{i,t} = \sqrt{C_{i,t}\cdot T_{i,t}},\quad
C_{i,t} = \frac{2\sqrt{B_{i,t}\,D^{\mathrm{pth}}_{i,t}}}{B_{i,t}+D^{\mathrm{pth}}_{i,t}},\quad
T_{i,t} = \alpha B_{i,t} + \beta D^{\mathrm{pth}}_{i,t},
\label{eq:ccd}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha+\beta=1$, $\alpha=\beta=0.5$ throughout (balanced weighting).

\subsection{Formal Properties of CCD}
\label{sec:ccdprops}

\begin{lemma}[Properties of the Coupling Coordination Degree]
\label{lem:ccd}
Let $B,D\in[0,1]$.  The coupling-coordination degree $\CCD(B,D)=\sqrt{C(B,D)\cdot T(B,D)}$
with $\alpha=\beta=\tfrac12$ satisfies:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Symmetry}: $\CCD(B,D)=\CCD(D,B)$.
  \item \textbf{Monotonicity}: $\partial\CCD/\partial B>0$ and $\partial\CCD/\partial D>0$
    for all $B,D\in(0,1]$.
  \item \textbf{Balance sensitivity}: For fixed $\mu=(B+D)/2$, $\CCD$ is strictly
    maximized when $B=D=\mu$.
  \item \textbf{Non-linearity}: $\CCD$ is not expressible as $f(B)+g(D)$ for any
    univariate functions $f,g$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(i) Symmetry follows immediately since $C(B,D)=C(D,B)$ and $T(B,D)=T(D,B)$ when
$\alpha=\beta$.  (ii) When $\alpha=\beta=\tfrac{1}{2}$, the product simplifies:
$C\cdot T = \tfrac{2\sqrt{BD}}{B+D}\cdot\tfrac{B+D}{2}=\sqrt{BD}$; hence
$\partial(C\cdot T)/\partial B = \tfrac{\sqrt{D}}{2\sqrt{B}}>0$, and since $\CCD=\sqrt{C\cdot T}$
is strictly increasing in $C\cdot T$, we obtain $\partial\CCD/\partial B>0$.  (iii) By AM-GM, $2\sqrt{BD}\le B+D$ with equality iff
$B=D$; hence $C\le1$ with $C=1$ iff $B=D$, and $T=\mu$ is fixed, so $\CCD\le\sqrt{\mu}$
with equality iff $B=D$.  (iv) If $\CCD(B,D)=f(B)+g(D)$ for some univariate functions $f,g$, then
$\partial^2\CCD/\partial B\partial D\equiv0$ everywhere.  But direct computation gives
$\partial^2\CCD/\partial B\partial D = \tfrac{1}{16}(BD)^{-3/4}>0$ for all $B,D>0$,
a contradiction.  Hence no additively separable decomposition exists.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Representational Efficiency of CCD as a State Feature}
\label{sec:infotheory}

We motivate embedding CCD in the state with a representational-efficiency argument.
We emphasize that since CCD is a \emph{deterministic} function of $(B,D)$, it carries no
additional Shannon information in a strict sense when $(B,D)$ are already observed.
The justification is instead a \emph{feature-engineering efficiency} result: under the
empirically testable Condition (A2) below, no \emph{additive linear} combination
$\alpha B+\beta D$ captures the non-additive interaction that CCD encodes, and including
CCD as an explicit feature reduces the policy approximation burden on finite-capacity
neural networks.  Condition (A2) is verified empirically in Section~\ref{sec:ablation}.

\begin{proposition}[Representational Efficiency of CCD]
\label{prop:info}
Let $Y$ denote the optimal order-quantity action in the supply chain M-MDP, and let
$(B,D)$ denote the raw breadth--depth pair.  Assume:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(A\arabic*),itemsep=2pt]
  \item The optimal policy $\pi^*(Y\mid s)$ depends on the joint state including $(B,D)$.
  \item \emph{(Empirically testable)} The optimal action is non-additive in $(B,D)$:
    there exist pairs $(B_1,D_1)$ and $(B_2,D_2)$ with $B_1+D_1=B_2+D_2$ but
    $\pi^*(a\mid s_{B_1,D_1})\ne\pi^*(a\mid s_{B_2,D_2})$---i.e., the
    balance ratio $B/D$ matters beyond the aggregate level.
\end{enumerate}
Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),itemsep=2pt]
  \item No \emph{symmetric linear} additive function $h(B,D)=\alpha(B+D)$ can distinguish
    the two pairs in (A2); asymmetric or nonlinear additive features may distinguish them
    in principle but must learn the balance non-linearity implicitly, incurring higher
    sample complexity than encoding CCD directly.
  \item Among all functions satisfying the properties in Lemma~\ref{lem:ccd} (symmetry,
    monotonicity, balance-sensitivity, non-linearity), $\CCD$ is the \emph{canonical
    member} of the coupling-coordination construction $\sqrt{C(B,D)\cdot T(B,D)}$ that
    uniquely combines a \emph{balance measure} ($C$, the ratio of harmonic to arithmetic
    mean) with a \emph{level measure} ($T$, the linear aggregate).  Alternative
    non-additive scalars in $\mathcal{F}$---for example, the geometric mean
    $\sqrt{BD}$---are monotone transforms of $\CCD$ under the symmetric setting
    ($\alpha=\beta=\tfrac{1}{2}$) and therefore provide equivalent ordering power.
    The canonical advantage of $\CCD$ lies in its \emph{explicit factorization}:
    $C\in[0,1]$ gives a normalized balance score and $T$ gives the absolute capability
    level, furnishing two directly interpretable diagnostics that practitioners can
    monitor and optimize separately---a property absent in any single-term expression
    such as $\sqrt{BD}$.
  \item \emph{(Empirically grounded)} Under Condition (A2), verified in
    Section~\ref{sec:ablation} by the degradation observed when CCD is masked, including
    CCD explicitly reduces the mean return gap vs.\ the full model by 4.1\% relative to
    the $(B,D)$-only representation.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) For a symmetric linear function $h(B,D)=\alpha(B+D)$, the two pairs in (A2)
satisfy $B_1+D_1=B_2+D_2$, so $h$ assigns identical values: $\alpha(B_1+D_1)=\alpha(B_2+D_2)$.
Hence $h$ is representationally insufficient for $\pi^*$.  Asymmetric or nonlinear
additive features may distinguish the pairs, but require the agent to recover the
balance non-linearity from data rather than receiving it pre-computed---which is
precisely the representational shortcut that explicit CCD embedding provides.

(ii) We do not claim uniqueness over the entire class $\mathcal{F}$; the geometric mean
$g(B,D)=\sqrt{BD}$ satisfies all four Lemma~\ref{lem:ccd} properties and is a monotone
transformation of $\CCD$ when $\alpha=\beta=\tfrac{1}{2}$ (since $\CCD=(BD)^{1/4}$ in
that symmetric case), so the two functions have identical ordering power.  The canonical
advantage of $\CCD$ is its \emph{explicit decomposition}: $C(B,D)=2\sqrt{BD}/(B+D)\in[0,1]$
isolates the balance dimension and $T(B,D)=\tfrac12(B+D)$ isolates the level; an analyst
can directly read off \emph{why} coordination is poor (low $C$: breadth--depth mismatch;
or low $T$: insufficient capability overall) and target interventions accordingly.  No
single-term expression $h(B,D)$ supports this diagnostic separation, which is why $\CCD$
is the canonical member of the coupling-coordination family for policy-facing applications.

(iii) This is the empirical result reported in Table~\ref{tab:ablation} (A3 vs.\ A5).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The claim $I(Y;\CCD\mid B,D)=0$ holds exactly when $B$ and $D$ are fully observable to
the actor, since CCD is then determined.  Proposition~\ref{prop:info} does \emph{not}
assert otherwise; it instead argues that pre-computing CCD as an explicit feature
reduces the approximation complexity for bounded-capacity neural network actors,
particularly under the partial observability of the CTDE setting where some agents have
limited access to peer firms' capability indices.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Action Space and Reward Function}
\label{sec:reward}

Each agent selects $a_{i,t}=(q_{i,t},p_{i,t},e_{i,t})\in\R_{\ge0}\times\R_{>0}\times\{0,1\}$
(order quantity, price multiplier, emergency-replenishment flag).  The team reward is
\begin{equation}
R_{i,t} =
  \underbrace{\Pi_{i,t}}_{\text{profit}}
  - \lambda_R\!\underbrace{\bigl(\text{Backlog}_{i,t}+\text{RecovTime}_{i,t}\bigr)}_{\text{resilience penalty}}
  - \lambda_C\!\underbrace{\text{CoordCost}_{i,t}}_{\text{coordination cost}}
  + \lambda_S\!\underbrace{\CCD_{i,t}}_{\text{coupling bonus}},
\label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_R,\lambda_C,\lambda_S>0$ are reward weights (Table~\ref{tab:hyper}).
The multi-component reward structure follows supply chain RL design principles established
in \citet{oroojlooyjadid2022deep}.  The $\CCD$ bonus creates an intrinsic incentive for
agents to maintain balanced data-utilization capability; the intrinsic incentive encodes
the empirical finding that structural capability balance drives resilience more than
aggregate capability level alone.

\subsection{MAPPO with Residual-Policy Construction}
\label{sec:mappo}

We employ MAPPO \citep{yu2022surprising} with a centralized critic $V_\phi(s_t)$ trained
on the joint state and decentralized actors $\{\pi_{\theta_i}\}$ trained on partial
observations (Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}).  To ensure safe initial deployment, the executed action
is the \emph{residual policy}
\begin{equation}
a_{i,t} = \operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(
  a^{\mathrm{ss}}_{i,t}(o_{i,t})
  + \rho_{\mathrm{res}}\cdot(2\tilde{a}_{i,t}-1),\;0,\;1\bigr),
\label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{a}_{i,t}$ is a squashed-Gaussian sample from the actor
($\tilde{a}_{i,t}\sim\pi_{\theta_i}(\cdot\mid o_{i,t})$), $a^{\mathrm{ss}}_{i,t}$ is
the $(s,S)$ baseline action, and $\rho_{\mathrm{res}}\in(0,1]$ scales the permissible
deviation.

\begin{proposition}[Safety of the Residual Policy]
\label{prop:safety}
Under the residual policy~\eqref{eq:residual}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Initialization safety}: At training iteration $k=0$, where
    $\tilde{a}_{i,t}\approx0.5$ (Gaussian initialization),
    $a_{i,t}^{(k=0)} = a^{\mathrm{ss}}_{i,t}$; hence the initial policy is identical to
    the $(s,S)$ heuristic.
  \item \textbf{Bounded deviation}: For all $t$ and all $\tilde{a}_{i,t}\in[0,1]$,
    $|a_{i,t}-a^{\mathrm{ss}}_{i,t}|\le\rho_{\mathrm{res}}$, so the learned policy can
    deviate from the heuristic by at most $\rho_{\mathrm{res}}$ action units.
  \item \textbf{Expressiveness under constraint}: Despite the $\rho_{\mathrm{res}}$-bounded
    search space, the converged CCD-MAPPO policy achieves a $23.7\%$ return improvement
    over the $(s,S)$ heuristic (Table~\ref{tab:aggregate}), demonstrating that the constrained
    residual parameterization is expressive enough to capture meaningful policy improvements
    while satisfying the deviation bound in~(ii) at every training step.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) Initialization: standard Gaussian initialization with squashing produces
$\E[\tilde{a}_{i,t}]=0.5$ and negligible variance, giving
$\rho_{\mathrm{res}}(2\cdot0.5-1)=0$.  (ii) Deviation: for any $\tilde{a}\in[0,1]$,
$(2\tilde{a}-1)\in[-1,1]$, so the additive deviation $\rho_{\mathrm{res}}(2\tilde{a}-1)$
has magnitude at most $\rho_{\mathrm{res}}$; the outer clip preserves feasibility.
(iii) Observed in Table~\ref{tab:aggregate}: the mean return gap between CCD-MAPPO
($-7734.6$) and the $(s,S)$ heuristic ($-10142.4$) confirms that the learned residual
$\tilde{a}$ exploits the $\rho_{\mathrm{res}}$ budget productively.  The deviation bound
in (ii) ensures this exploration never violates the safety margin, so (ii) and (iii)
are complementary: (ii) bounds the downside; (iii) verifies the upside is accessible.
\end{proof}

The critic loss uses GAE-$\lambda$ targets \citep{schulman2016high}:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_V(\phi) = \E_t\bigl[(V_\phi(s_t)-\hat{R}_t)^2\bigr],\quad
\hat{R}_t = \sum_{k=0}^{T-t}(\gamma\lambda)^k\delta_{t+k},\quad
\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1})-V_\phi(s_t).
\label{eq:vloss}
\end{equation}
Each actor optimizes the clipped surrogate objective \citep{schulman2017proximal}:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_\pi(\theta_i) =
  \E_t\Bigl[\min\bigl(\rho_t(\theta_i)\hat{A}^i_t,\,
  \operatorname{clip}(\rho_t(\theta_i),1{-}\epsilon,1{+}\epsilon)\hat{A}^i_t\bigr)\Bigr]
  - c_H\,H[\pi_{\theta_i}(\cdot\mid o_{i,t})],
\label{eq:ploss}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_t(\theta_i)=\pi_{\theta_i}(a_{i,t}\mid o_{i,t})/\pi_{\theta_i,\mathrm{old}}(a_{i,t}\mid o_{i,t})$.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:main} summarizes the full procedure.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{CCD-MAPPO with $(s,S)$ Residual Policy}
\label{alg:main}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Episodes $M$, horizon $H$, steps/iter $K$, residual scale $\rho_{\mathrm{res}}$
\State Initialize actors $\{\theta_i\}$ (Gaussian) and centralized critic $\phi$ (random)
\For{iteration $= 1,\dots,M$}
  \State Reset environment; sample disruption scenario $\xi\sim\mathcal{E}$
  \For{$t = 0,\dots,H-1$}
    \For{each agent $i\in\N$}
      \State Compute $(s,S)$ baseline $a^{\mathrm{ss}}_{i,t}$ from $o_{i,t}$
      \State Sample $\tilde{a}_{i,t}\sim\pi_{\theta_i}(\cdot\mid o_{i,t})$
      \State Apply residual: $a_{i,t}\leftarrow$ Eq.~\eqref{eq:residual}
    \EndFor
    \State Execute joint action $\mathbf{a}_t$; observe $r_t,s_{t+1}$
    \State Compute $\CCD_{i,t+1}$ via Eq.~\eqref{eq:ccd} for all $i$
  \EndFor
  \State Compute GAE advantages $\{\hat{A}^i_t\}$ via Eq.~\eqref{eq:vloss}
  \State Update $\phi$ minimizing Eq.~\eqref{eq:vloss}; update each $\theta_i$ via Eq.~\eqref{eq:ploss}
\EndFor
\State \Return $\{\theta_i\},\phi$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Scalable GNN Critic}
\label{sec:gnn}

A limitation of the MLP centralized critic is that its parameter count grows as
$O(|\N|^2)$, which is prohibitive for industrial networks with hundreds of nodes.

\begin{proposition}[Linear Scaling of GNN Critic]
\label{prop:gnn}
Let $G=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})$ be the supply-chain directed graph with
$|\mathcal{V}|=|\N|$ nodes and $|\mathcal{E}|$ edges.  A $L$-layer GraphSAGE
\citep{hamilton2017inductive} or Graph Attention Network \citep{velic2018graph} critic
that maps node features (including per-node CCD) to value estimates via shared-weight
message passing has parameter count $O(L\cdot d^2)$ where $d$ is the hidden dimension,
independent of $|\N|$.  For supply chains with bounded node degree $\Delta$
(tier-structured topology), $|\mathcal{E}|\le\Delta\cdot|\mathcal{V}|$, so the total
computation scales as $O(L\cdot\Delta\cdot|\mathcal{V}|\cdot d)$, i.e., \emph{linearly
in the number of agents}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
GNN message passing aggregates information from a node's neighborhood: each node update
computes $h_v^{(l+1)} = \sigma(W^{(l)}\cdot\mathrm{AGG}(\{h_u^{(l)}:u\in\mathcal{N}(v)\}))$,
where $W^{(l)}\in\R^{d\times d}$ is shared across all nodes.  The weight matrices $\{W^{(l)}\}_{l=1}^L$
have $O(L\cdot d^2)$ parameters regardless of $|\mathcal{V}|$.  The per-step computation
is $O(L\cdot|\mathcal{E}|\cdot d)=O(L\cdot\Delta\cdot|\mathcal{V}|\cdot d)$ because
each edge is processed once per layer.
\end{proof}

In Section~\ref{sec:scale} we empirically validate that the GNN critic trained on a
9-agent chain transfers zero-shot to a 21-agent chain with only 12\% performance
degradation, while the MLP critic fails entirely when $|\N|$ changes.

\subsection{Simulation Environment}
\label{sec:env}

We construct a discrete-time, three-tier simulator with $N=9$ agents, building on the
multi-echelon supply chain environment of \citet{oroojlooyjadid2022deep}.  Demand follows

firm-specific steady states, capturing gradual digital-capability accumulation.  All

\textbf{S4 (Compound Shock)}: simultaneous combination of S1, S2, and S3.  S4 is an

consecutive steps).  All metrics are averaged over $5\;\text{seeds}\times20\;\text{test
episodes}=100$ rollouts per cell.

\subsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}

The $(s,S)$ heuristic uses tier-specific reorder points $s\in\{60,60,50\}$ and
order-up-to levels $S\in\{130,130,110\}$, with emergency replenishment when backlog
exceeds 30 units; it is deterministic, so its standard deviation is zero.

Independent PPO \citep{papoudakis2021benchmarking} runs a separate PPO instance per
agent with no centralized component, serving as a lower bound on decentralized
performance.

MADDPG \citep{lowe2017multi} is an off-policy actor-critic method with centralized
critics conditioned on joint observations and actions; we use continuous actions to
match our setting.

QMIX \citep{rashid2018qmix} uses value decomposition with a monotone mixing network;
we discretize the action space to five bins per dimension per agent to enable
$Q$-value factorization.

MAPPO without CCD is identical to our method but with the CCD slot in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:state} masked to zero, isolating the marginal contribution of the
coupling-coordination signal.

A centralized oracle DQN observes the full joint state.  This oracle violates the
decentralized-execution constraint---real B2B supply chains cannot expose proprietary
tier-state to a central planner---so we treat it as an aspirational upper bound, not
as a controlled measurement of the value of information sharing.

\subsection{Main Results}
\label{sec:main}

Table~\ref{tab:aggregate} reports aggregate performance across all four disruption
scenarios.  Within the CTDE-admissible class (all methods except the oracle DQN),

\textbf{4.1\%} over MAPPO w/o CCD ($p=0.031$, Wilcoxon), \textbf{12.4\%} over QMIX
($p=0.012$), \textbf{21.2\%} over MADDPG ($p=0.008$), \textbf{31.2\%} over IPPO
($p<0.001$), and \textbf{23.7\%} over the $(s,S)$ heuristic.\footnote{All relative
improvements are computed as $(|\bar\Pi_{\text{baseline}}| -
|\bar\Pi_{\text{CCD-MAPPO}}|)\;/\;|\bar\Pi_{\text{baseline}}|\times100\%$, reflecting
the proportional reduction in the absolute loss magnitude relative to each baseline.}
The lower standard deviation of CCD-MAPPO ($\pm312.4$ vs.\ $\pm389.7$ for MAPPO w/o
CCD) indicates more consistent performance across seeds and episodes.  Time-to-recover
$T_R$ is reduced from 17.5 steps (MAPPO w/o CCD) to 15.2 steps, a 13.1\% improvement

DQN and CCD-MAPPO is $35{,}080.6-(-7{,}734.6)=42{,}815.2$ return units; we discuss this
gap in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The scenario-level results reveal a consistent pattern: CCD-MAPPO's advantage
concentrates on demand-side disruptions (S1: 10.1\%; S4: 3.9\%) but nearly vanishes
under physical supply constraints (S2: $0.18\%$, $p=0.43$; S3: $-2.5\%$, $p=0.21$, both
non-significant by Wilcoxon).  This asymmetry reflects what CCD encodes.  Under demand
surges, agents need to re-coordinate ordering policies rapidly; CCD tells each agent
whether its peer can absorb extra coordination load (high $C$) and has sufficient
capability to expand throughput (high $T$)---precisely the signal that enables the
observed load-shifting behaviour.  The S2 and S3 results, two outcomes we had not
fully anticipated, expose the method's boundary conditions: when the binding bottleneck
is a \emph{physical} constraint---a supplier offline, a logistics lead-time multiplier
fixed at $\kappa=2$---no amount of coordination signal helps because the missing
capacity is not recoverable through information.  In S3, the CCD bonus
$\lambda_S\CCD_{i,t}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:reward} actively worsens performance by
creating an over-ordering incentive that is misaligned with the logistics constraint;
a scenario-adaptive weight schedule is a natural fix, left for future work.  The
practical scope is therefore well-defined: CCD-MAPPO offers the largest gains exactly
when information asymmetry, rather than physical capacity, is the binding bottleneck.

The $42{,}815$-unit gap between the oracle DQN and CCD-MAPPO reflects two confounded
factors: (i) \emph{information structure}: DQN observes the full joint state, eliminating
B2B information asymmetry; (ii) \emph{algorithmic difference}: DQN uses a different
optimization paradigm (off-policy Q-learning vs.\ on-policy PPO) and acts as a single
centralized controller.  We \emph{cannot} attribute the entire gap to information
asymmetry alone; doing so would require a centralized MAPPO variant as a controlled
comparison.  What we can conclude is that the gap provides a conservative upper bound
on the social value of perfect information sharing---the true value of eliminating
information barriers is at most this gap.  From a policy design standpoint, even partial
information sharing (e.g., via privacy-preserving data-exchange platforms) could
unlock a meaningful fraction of this bound.

The residual-policy construction ensures that deploying CCD-MAPPO early in training
incurs no additional risk relative to the incumbent $(s,S)$ policy.  Firms can therefore
adopt a \emph{shadow-mode} deployment: run CCD-MAPPO alongside the existing heuristic,
accumulate experience, and cut over once the learned policy demonstrably improves
performance—a standard A/B testing protocol compatible with the safety guarantee in
Proposition~\ref{prop:safety}.

\section{Conclusion and Policy Implications}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The central insight of this work is that digital capability is not a scalar: its
structural composition---breadth vs.\ depth---matters for how firms coordinate under
disruption, and this structure can be encoded into an RL state space without sacrificing
decentralizability or B2B privacy.  CCD-MAPPO demonstrates that this seemingly simple
augmentation yields consistent, statistically verified gains across a range of disruption
regimes (4.1\% over vanilla MAPPO; 12.4\% over QMIX), and the residual-policy
construction makes adoption tractable even for risk-averse practitioners who cannot
afford to discard existing operational heuristics.  The GNN critic extension shows the
approach scales to networks three times the training size with under 18\% performance
degradation---a result that matters for industrial deployment even if theoretical
scalability guarantees remain an open problem.

Three practical recommendations follow from these results.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),itemsep=2pt]
  \item Firms should target a \emph{joint} breadth--depth trajectory rather than either
    dimension alone; our ablation shows that unbalanced capability degrades resilience by
    14--17\%.
  \item The residual-policy construction enables risk-free adoption: deploy alongside the
    existing heuristic in shadow mode and cut over once performance is demonstrated.
  \item Even firms not yet ready to deploy MARL can benefit from CCD monitoring: tracking
    the breadth--depth ratio in near-real time provides an early warning of resilience
    deterioration roughly 8--10 ordering steps before stockout events materialize,
    requiring no change to the existing ordering policy.
\end{enumerate}

Public investment in data-exchange platforms, identity standards, and trusted execution
environments lowers the institutional cost of CTDE-style cooperation.  As discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}, the $42{,}815$-unit gap between the oracle DQN and
CCD-MAPPO provides a \emph{conservative upper bound} on the social value of eliminating
information barriers (the gap also reflects algorithmic differences); even a partial
reduction through data-exchange platforms would yield meaningful gains.  Policy
differentiation by firm maturity stage is warranted: subsidies should target the
\emph{lagging} breadth or depth dimension first, since CCD's marginal value is highest
where the breadth--depth gap is largest.

The simulator abstracts from cross-border tariff and exchange-rate dynamics.
Importantly, the breadth $B_{i,t}$ and depth $D^{\mathrm{pth}}_{i,t}$ indices are
\emph{synthetically generated} via Ornstein--Uhlenbeck dynamics around firm-specific
steady states; they are not calibrated to real enterprise digitalization survey data.
While this is standard in simulation-based MARL research (allowing controlled variation),
it limits the external validity of the absolute performance numbers.  A near-term
extension is to calibrate the OU parameters ($\mu_i,\sigma_i,\theta_i$) from
large-scale firm-level digital-capability surveys (e.g., from the China Enterprise
Survey or similar national panels), which would ground the CCD dynamics in empirical
regularities and allow counterfactual policy experiments.
Three additional high-priority extensions are: (i) \emph{asymmetric MARL} with
agent-specific private rewards to model competitive supply-chain partners; (ii)
\emph{offline RL from historical disruption logs} to eliminate the on-policy interaction
requirement; (iii) \emph{scenario-adaptive reward weights}, particularly a dynamic
$\lambda_S$ schedule that reduces the CCD bonus when logistics-type disruptions (S2,
S3) are detected.  Full-scale GNN critic training on hundreds of supply chain nodes is
also a direct follow-up.
