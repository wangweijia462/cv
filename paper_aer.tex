% I. INTRODUCTION
% ============================================================================

\section{Introduction}

The economic consequences of interstate conflict have long occupied a central place in macroeconomics, yet structural models capable of quantifying the full general-equilibrium effects of modern warfare remain scarce. \citet{federle2026price} provide the most comprehensive empirical assessment to date, documenting that wars reduce GDP in the war site by approximately 10 percent and generate significant negative spillovers to third countries through bilateral trade linkages. Their reduced-form local projection estimates, however, cannot speak to counterfactual policy interventions or disentangle the multiple channels through which a specific conflict scenario would propagate through the global economy.

This paper fills that gap. We construct a four-region New Keynesian DSGE model calibrated to a concrete, policy-relevant scenario: a US--Israel military strike on Iran followed by an Iranian blockade of the Strait of Hormuz. The Hormuz Strait handles approximately 20 percent of global oil trade \citep{eia2023hormuz}, making its disruption a uniquely potent transmission mechanism that extends far beyond the bilateral trade channels emphasized in the existing literature.

Our model makes three contributions. First, we embed the trade-exposure spillover mechanism documented by \citet{federle2026price} within a structural DSGE framework that permits explicit counterfactual analysis. The model features four regions---Iran (war site), the US--Israel bloc (belligerent), China (stabilizer), and the rest of the world (ROW)---each governed by a New Keynesian Phillips curve, an IS curve, and a Taylor rule, linked through a global oil market and bilateral trade flows.

Second, we extend the real-side analysis with financial channels that prove quantitatively important. Sovereign risk premia, capital flows governed by an uncovered interest parity condition, equity market valuations, and a financial contagion matrix all interact with the real economy. The inclusion of financial channels amplifies ROW output losses from 1.1 percent (trade channel only) to 3.8 percent (trade plus financial channels), consistent with the empirical evidence on financial transmission of geopolitical shocks \citep{caldara2022measuring}.

Third, we introduce and quantify a novel mechanism: the stabilization role of a large emerging economy during a global crisis. China's triple stabilization---strategic petroleum reserve (SPR) releases, accelerated renewable energy substitution, and manufacturing export expansion to fill disrupted supply chains---reduces cumulative global GDP losses by approximately \$2.2 trillion and lowers peak global inflation by 0.29 percentage points. This finding speaks to an underexplored dimension of the ``China shock'' literature: rather than disrupting advanced-economy labor markets \citep{autor2013china}, China may serve as a global economic ballast during geopolitical crises.

Our baseline results indicate that under a 70 percent Hormuz blockade, Iran experiences a peak GDP decline of 27.2 percent---substantially larger than the average war-site effect in \citet{federle2026price}, reflecting Iran's extreme dependence on oil export revenue. The US--Israel bloc experiences a modest GDP increase of 1.7 percent driven by military Keynesian stimulus, though consumption welfare declines. Global oil prices surge by 63 percent at peak, and the financial contagion channel generates sovereign spread increases of 1,403 basis points for Iran, with meaningful spillovers to emerging market spreads worldwide.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:literature} reviews related literature. Section~\ref{sec:model} presents the DSGE model. Section~\ref{sec:calibration} discusses calibration. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the main results, including sensitivity analysis and welfare calculations. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes with policy implications.

\section{Related Literature}
\label{sec:literature}

This paper relates to several strands of the literature.

\textit{The economics of war and conflict.} A large literature studies the macroeconomic effects of armed conflict. Early contributions focused on the war site: \citet{abadie2003economic} use synthetic control methods to estimate the cost of the Basque conflict, while \citet{barro2006rare} models wars as rare disasters affecting asset prices. More recently, \citet{federle2026price} provide a comprehensive cross-country panel analysis using local projections \citep{jorda2005estimation} to estimate the dynamic effects of wars on GDP, inflation, and trade. They identify wars through a narrative approach, distinguishing war sites from belligerents and third countries, and document that bilateral trade exposure is the primary channel through which war-related GDP losses propagate internationally. Our model takes their empirical findings as a calibration target and embeds them in a structural framework that permits counterfactual policy analysis.

\textit{DSGE models of geopolitical risk.} The application of DSGE models to conflict scenarios is relatively recent. \citet{caldara2022measuring} construct a geopolitical risk index and study its macroeconomic effects, finding significant impacts on investment and output. \citet{bodenstein2011oil} develop a two-country DSGE model with oil trade to study the transmission of oil supply shocks, providing a methodological foundation for our multi-region energy channel. Our contribution extends this literature by integrating military shocks, trade disruption, energy supply shocks, and financial contagion within a single unified framework calibrated to a specific conflict scenario.

\textit{Oil shocks and the macroeconomy.} The macroeconomic effects of oil price shocks have been extensively studied since \citet{hamilton1983oil}. \citet{blanchard2007macroeconomic} analyze why recent oil shocks have had smaller macroeconomic effects than those in the 1970s, attributing the change to improved monetary policy and reduced energy intensity. \citet{kilian2009not} emphasizes the distinction between supply-driven and demand-driven oil price changes. Our model features an explicit global oil market where the Hormuz blockade creates a supply-side shock with endogenous price determination, SPR policy responses, and heterogeneous regional exposure.

\textit{Financial contagion and sovereign risk.} The financial channels in our model build on the literature on sovereign risk premia and capital flow dynamics. \citet{longstaff2011financial} study contagion in sovereign credit default swap markets, while \citet{rey2015dilemma} documents the global financial cycle driven by US monetary policy and risk appetite. Our financial contagion matrix captures the cross-regional transmission of sovereign spreads, with amplification through a global risk aversion factor that responds to capital flight from the conflict zone.

\textit{China's role in the global economy.} The literature on China's global economic impact has largely focused on the ``China shock'' in trade \citep{autor2013china} and China's role in global imbalances \citep{bernanke2005global}. More recent work examines China's strategic petroleum reserves \citep{zhang2020china} and industrial policy in renewable energy \citep{helveston2019china}. We contribute a novel perspective by modeling China not as a source of disruption but as a potential stabilizer during global crises---a role enabled by its large SPR, expanding renewable capacity, and diversified manufacturing base.

\section{The Model}
\label{sec:model}

We develop a four-region New Keynesian DSGE model in which regions are linked through a global oil market, bilateral trade, and financial flows. Each region $i \in \{\text{Iran}, \text{US-Israel}, \text{China}, \text{ROW}\}$ is characterized by a representative household, a continuum of monopolistically competitive firms, and a central bank.

\subsection{Regional Equilibrium}

Each region solves a standard New Keynesian three-equation system, augmented with energy, trade, and financial channels.

\subsubsection{New Keynesian Phillips Curve}

The supply side of each region is governed by:
\begin{equation}
\pi_{i,t} = \beta \, \mathbb{E}_t[\pi_{i,t+1}] + \kappa_i \, \hat{y}_{i,t-1} + \xi^{\text{supply}}_{i,t}
\label{eq:nkpc}
\end{equation}
where $\pi_{i,t}$ is inflation, $\hat{y}_{i,t}$ is the output gap, $\beta$ is the discount factor, and $\kappa_i$ is the region-specific slope of the Phillips curve:
\begin{equation}
\kappa_i = \frac{(1-\theta_i)(1-\beta\theta_i)}{\theta_i} \cdot \frac{\sigma_i + \phi_i}{1 + \epsilon_i \phi_i}
\label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
with $\theta_i$ the Calvo price stickiness parameter, $\sigma_i$ the coefficient of relative risk aversion, $\phi_i$ the Frisch elasticity inverse, and $\epsilon_i$ the elasticity of substitution across varieties. The supply shock $\xi^{\text{supply}}_{i,t}$ combines energy cost pass-through and physical destruction:
\begin{equation}
\xi^{\text{supply}}_{i,t} = \alpha_{e,i} \cdot \max(\Delta p^{\text{oil}}_t, 0) \cdot 0.6 + \delta^{\text{destroy}}_{i,t} \cdot 0.8 - \chi^{\text{mfg}}_t \cdot \omega_i \cdot 0.2
\label{eq:supply_shock}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_{e,i}$ is the energy share in production, $\delta^{\text{destroy}}_{i,t}$ is physical capital destruction (nonzero only for Iran), and $\chi^{\text{mfg}}_t$ is China's manufacturing substitution effect with $\omega_i$ measuring trade openness.

\subsubsection{IS Curve with Financial Conditions}

The demand side features an IS curve augmented with military spending, trade disruption, energy costs, and financial conditions:
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}_{i,t} = \rho \, \hat{y}_{i,t-1} - \frac{1}{\sigma_i}(i_{i,t} - \mathbb{E}_t[\pi_{i,t+1}]) + \phi^{\text{mil}} g^{\text{mil}}_{i,t} - \alpha_{e,i} \cdot \max(\Delta p^{\text{oil}}_t, 0) \cdot 0.3 - \tau^{\text{trade}}_{i,t} + \Phi^{\text{fin}}_{i,t}
\label{eq:is}
\end{equation}
where $\rho = 0.75$ captures output persistence, $g^{\text{mil}}_{i,t}$ is the military spending impulse (with multiplier $\phi^{\text{mil}} = 0.8$), $\tau^{\text{trade}}_{i,t}$ captures trade disruption effects, and $\Phi^{\text{fin}}_{i,t}$ is the financial conditions index:
\begin{equation}
\Phi^{\text{fin}}_{i,t} = -\gamma_s \cdot s_{i,t} - \gamma_k \cdot \max(-k_{i,t}, 0)
\label{eq:fci}
\end{equation}
with $\gamma_s = 0.15$ and $\gamma_k = 0.10$ governing the sensitivity of output to sovereign spreads $s_{i,t}$ and capital outflows $k_{i,t}$, respectively.

\subsubsection{Taylor Rule}

Monetary policy follows a standard Taylor rule with interest rate smoothing:
\begin{equation}
i_{i,t} = \rho_{i,i} \, i_{i,t-1} + (1 - \rho_{i,i})(\phi_{\pi,i} \, \pi_{i,t} + \phi_{y,i} \, \hat{y}_{i,t-1})
\label{eq:taylor}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_{i,i}$ is the smoothing parameter, $\phi_{\pi,i}$ is the inflation response coefficient, and $\phi_{y,i}$ is the output gap response coefficient.


\subsection{Global Oil Market}

The Strait of Hormuz handles approximately 20 percent of global petroleum trade. An Iranian blockade disrupts transit for all producers routing through the strait, including Saudi Arabia (12 percent of global supply), the UAE (4 percent), Iraq (5 percent), and Iran itself (4 percent).

The global oil supply shock at time $t$ is:
\begin{equation}
\Delta S_t = \underbrace{\lambda^{\text{block}}_t \cdot (s^{\text{Hormuz}} - s^{\text{Iran}})}_{\text{Transit disruption}} + \underbrace{s^{\text{Iran}} \cdot \lambda^{\text{prod}}}_{\text{Iran production loss}} - \underbrace{0.3 \cdot \lambda^{\text{block}}_t \cdot (s^{\text{Hormuz}} - s^{\text{Iran}})}_{\text{Pipeline rerouting offset}}
\label{eq:oil_supply}
\end{equation}
where $s^{\text{Hormuz}} = 0.20$ is Hormuz's share of global supply, $s^{\text{Iran}} = 0.04$ is Iran's production share, $\lambda^{\text{block}}_t$ is blockade severity (decaying over time), and $\lambda^{\text{prod}} = 0.80$ is the fraction of Iranian production destroyed.

Oil prices are determined by supply-demand balance with low short-run elasticities:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\Delta P^{\text{oil}}_t}{P^{\text{oil}}} = \frac{\Delta S_t - \text{SPR}_t + \Delta D_t}{|\varepsilon_d| + \varepsilon_s}
\label{eq:oil_price}
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon_d = -0.05$ and $\varepsilon_s = 0.10$ are short-run demand and supply elasticities \citep{hamilton2009understanding}, $\text{SPR}_t$ is aggregate strategic reserve releases, and $\Delta D_t$ captures demand destruction from the recession.

\subsection{Financial Channels}

We augment the model with three financial variables per region, capturing channels that proved quantitatively important in the 2022 Russia--Ukraine crisis.

\subsubsection{Sovereign Risk Premium}

The sovereign spread follows:
\begin{equation}
s_{i,t} = \underbrace{s^{\text{shock}}_{i,t}}_{\text{Exogenous}} + \underbrace{0.3 \cdot \max(-\hat{y}_{i,t-1}, 0)}_{\text{Countercyclical}} + \underbrace{0.2 \cdot s_{i,t-1}}_{\text{Persistence}}
\label{eq:spread}
\end{equation}
where $s^{\text{shock}}_{i,t}$ is the region-specific exogenous risk premium shock. For Iran, this takes the form $s^{\text{shock}}_{\text{Iran},t} = 0.05 \cdot e^{-0.08t}$, reflecting a 500 basis point initial jump with gradual decay.

\subsubsection{Capital Flows}

Net capital inflows are determined by an augmented uncovered interest parity (UIP) condition:
\begin{equation}
k_{i,t} = k^{\text{shock}}_{i,t} - 2.0 \cdot s_{i,t} + 0.5 \cdot (i_{i,t} - \bar{r}) + 0.3 \cdot k_{i,t-1}
\label{eq:capital}
\end{equation}
where the spread coefficient of $-2.0$ captures the strong negative effect of sovereign risk on capital inflows, and $\bar{r} = 0.02$ is the global risk-free rate. This specification generates capital flight from Iran and safe-haven flows into the US, consistent with typical crisis dynamics.

\subsubsection{Equity Markets}

Equity valuations follow a simplified Gordon growth model:
\begin{equation}
q_{i,t} = 0.6 \cdot q_{i,t-1} + 10 \cdot g^e_{i,t} - 8 \cdot (i_{i,t} + s_{i,t}) - 3 \cdot \max(-\hat{y}_{i,t}, 0) + q^{\text{shock}}_{i,t}
\label{eq:equity}
\end{equation}
where $g^e_{i,t} = 0.02 + 0.5 \cdot \hat{y}_{i,t}$ is expected earnings growth and the discount rate is the sum of the policy rate and sovereign spread.

\subsubsection{Real Exchange Rate}

The real exchange rate responds to spreads, capital flows, and policy intervention:
\begin{equation}
e_{i,t} = 0.4 \cdot e_{i,t-1} + 0.5 \cdot s_{i,t} - 0.3 \cdot k_{i,t} + \iota^{\text{fx}}_{i,t}
\label{eq:rer}
\end{equation}
where $\iota^{\text{fx}}_{i,t}$ captures foreign exchange intervention (nonzero only for China in the stabilization scenario).

\subsubsection{Financial Contagion}

Sovereign spreads transmit across regions through a financial linkage matrix $\mathbf{L}$:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{s}^{\text{shock}}_{j,t} = s^{\text{shock}}_{j,t} + 0.1 \cdot \left(\sum_{i \neq j} L_{ij} \cdot s_{i,t-1}\right) \cdot \Gamma_t
\label{eq:contagion}
\end{equation}
where $L_{ij}$ measures financial linkage from region $i$ to region $j$, and $\Gamma_t = 1 + 2 \cdot \max(-k_{\text{Iran},t}, 0)$ is a global risk aversion amplifier that increases during periods of Iranian capital flight. The linkage matrix is calibrated to reflect the empirical pattern of financial contagion, with Iran having limited direct linkages but generating amplified indirect effects through the global risk channel.


\subsection{China's Triple Stabilization Mechanism}

China's stabilization operates through three distinct channels, each with different temporal profiles.

\textit{Strategic Petroleum Reserve Release.} China releases SPR stocks at rate:
\begin{equation}
\text{SPR}^{\text{China}}_t = \begin{cases} 0.008 \cdot e^{-0.1t} + 0.002(1-e^{-0.15t}) & t < 8 \\ 0.002 \cdot e^{-0.2(t-8)} + 0.002(1-e^{-0.15t}) & t \geq 8 \end{cases}
\label{eq:spr}
\end{equation}
where the first term represents direct SPR drawdown and the second captures renewable energy substitution that progressively displaces oil demand. China's SPR capacity parameter is calibrated at 0.8, reflecting approximately 90 days of import cover.

\textit{Renewable Energy Substitution.} The renewable offset $\Delta^{\text{ren}}_t = 0.002(1-e^{-0.15t})$ grows over time as China accelerates deployment of solar, wind, and electric vehicle adoption in response to elevated oil prices, gradually reducing aggregate oil demand.

\textit{Manufacturing Export Expansion.} China expands manufacturing output to fill global supply gaps:
\begin{equation}
\chi^{\text{mfg}}_t = 0.015 \cdot (1 - e^{-0.2t})
\label{eq:mfg}
\end{equation}
This channel operates through the supply shock term in the Phillips curve of trading partners, reducing imported inflation by providing substitute goods for disrupted supply chains.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We simulate the model over 32 quarters (8 years) and report results for two versions: (i) the trade-channel-only model, and (ii) the full model with financial channels. We then analyze China's stabilization role and perform sensitivity analysis across war severity scenarios.

\subsection{Baseline Impulse Responses}

Figure~\ref{fig:impulse} presents the impulse response functions for the six core macroeconomic variables across all four regions.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/figure2_impulse_responses.png}
\caption{Impulse Responses to War Onset}
\label{fig:impulse}
\begin{minipage}{\textwidth}
\vspace{0.3em}
\small \textit{Notes:} Impulse responses to a war of baseline intensity (Hormuz blockade severity 70 percent). Shaded areas indicate 90 percent confidence bands from parameter uncertainty. Horizontal axis: years after war onset.
\end{minipage}
\end{figure}

\textit{Iran (war site).} Iran suffers the most severe impact, with GDP declining 27.2 percent at trough (Panel A). This substantially exceeds the average war-site effect of $-10$ percent documented by \citet{federle2026price}, reflecting three reinforcing mechanisms: physical capital destruction, trade disruption from the blockade, and---uniquely---the loss of oil export revenue that constitutes 30 percent of Iran's economic activity. Consumer prices surge 7.2 percentage points (Panel B) despite the output collapse, reflecting a classic stagflation pattern driven by supply-side destruction. Investment collapses by over 35 percent (Panel C) as both physical destruction and soaring risk premia deter capital formation.

\textit{US-Israel (belligerent).} The US-Israel bloc experiences a modest positive GDP effect of 1.7 percent (Panel A), driven by military Keynesian stimulus of 2.5 percent of GDP. This is consistent with \citeauthor{federle2026price}'s finding that belligerent countries experience near-zero average GDP effects, with military spending roughly offsetting the costs of war. However, consumer prices rise by 1.5 percentage points (Panel B) due to the global oil price increase, and the Federal Reserve responds with rate increases of 2.6 percentage points (Panel D). The welfare implications of this apparent GDP ``gain'' are negative, as we show in Section~\ref{sec:welfare}.

\textit{China (stabilizer).} China experiences a contained GDP decline of 1.2 percent, despite being a major oil importer ($\alpha_e = 0.06$). The stabilization mechanisms---SPR releases, renewable substitution, and manufacturing expansion---moderate what would otherwise be a larger negative impact. Inflation rises by 2.4 percentage points, the highest among non-war-site regions, reflecting China's energy-intensive industrial structure.

\textit{Rest of World.} ROW GDP declines by 3.8 percent in the full model with financial channels, significantly exceeding the 1.1 percent decline under trade channels alone. This amplification, analyzed in detail in Section~\ref{sec:financial}, demonstrates the quantitative importance of financial transmission.

\textit{Oil prices.} Panel E shows global oil prices surging 63 percent at peak under the baseline scenario. The comparison between simulations with and without China's stabilization reveals a meaningful dampening effect, with the peak approximately 5 percentage points lower when China intervenes.

% ============================================================================
% VI. CONCLUSION
% ============================================================================

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper develops a four-region New Keynesian DSGE model to analyze the macroeconomic consequences of a US--Israel conflict with Iran featuring a Hormuz Strait blockade. By embedding the trade-exposure spillover mechanism of \citet{federle2026price} within a structural framework augmented with financial channels, we make three contributions to the literature on the economics of war.

First, we demonstrate that the Hormuz chokepoint creates a uniquely potent transmission mechanism. The energy price channel dominates bilateral trade as the primary spillover vector, generating global oil price increases of 63 percent and GDP losses that extend far beyond Iran's direct trading partners. Iran's peak GDP loss of 27.2 percent---substantially larger than the average war-site effect in \citeauthor{federle2026price}---reflects the compounding of physical destruction with the loss of oil export revenue.

Second, we show that financial channels amplify the real-side effects asymmetrically. ROW output losses increase by a factor of 3.45$\times$ when financial contagion is incorporated, while China's managed capital account and active intervention insulate it from amplification. This finding contributes to the debate on capital account management and macroprudential policy during geopolitical crises.

Third, and most novel, we quantify China's role as a global economic stabilizer. China's triple mechanism---SPR releases, renewable energy substitution, and manufacturing export expansion---reduces cumulative global GDP losses by \$2.2 trillion and lowers peak global inflation by 0.29 percentage points. This finding challenges the prevailing narrative that frames China's global economic role primarily in terms of competitive disruption. In the context of a major geopolitical crisis, China's large strategic reserves, diversified energy mix, and manufacturing capacity enable it to serve as a ``global economic ballast,'' dampening the macroeconomic volatility that would otherwise propagate through trade and financial channels.

Several caveats apply. Our model is solved by sequential linearization rather than full nonlinear methods, which may understate nonlinearities in extreme scenarios. The financial linkage matrix is calibrated rather than estimated, and the welfare calculations abstract from distributional effects. Future work could embed our framework in an estimated Bayesian DSGE model with richer financial frictions, endogenous conflict dynamics, and heterogeneous agents.

The policy implications are clear. The Strait of Hormuz remains a critical vulnerability for the global economy, and military planners should incorporate the full general-equilibrium costs---including financial contagion and third-country spillovers---into strategic assessments. For China, the results suggest that investments in strategic reserves, renewable energy capacity, and manufacturing resilience generate positive externalities for global stability that extend well beyond their domestic benefits. For the international community, the findings underscore the importance of multilateral SPR coordination and energy diversification as insurance against geopolitical supply disruptions.
